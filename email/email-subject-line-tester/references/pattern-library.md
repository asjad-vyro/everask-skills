# Pattern library

All statistics here are vendor-reported by email-tool companies and unaudited: trust direction, never magnitude. Example subjects show style only; they are not facts about the user's business and must not be reused as the user's content.

## B2B cold outbound

Short, plain, anchored to the recipient's world.

**Length**: 1 to 4 words, at most about 35 characters. Mobile clients truncate around 30 to 35 characters. Several independent vendor datasets agree that shorter wins, so this is the best-supported rule.

**Angles** (one per variant), in default order of replies bought per research minute:
1. **Internal-style plain noun**: reads like a colleague's note, not a pitch (style: "reply rates", "trial delays"). Near-zero research, reusable across the whole list.
2. **Specific pain question**: names the persona's exact pain (style: "losing ramp time?"). Generic questions ("Quick question?") fail.
3. **Executive-world anchor**: the executive's own priority or risk, never the sender's product.
4. **Competitor or market reference**: needs a pass over each account.
5. **Company initiative**: a public project the account owns; same cost as the competitor angle.
6. **Trigger event**: something that just happened at their company; highest ceiling, but sourced per prospect and stale fast.

Re-rank to the user's situation and say which fact moved which angle: an executive-only list moves the executive anchor above the pain question; no research budget or no trigger data deletes angles 4 to 6 (name them, draft fewer variants, never park them at the bottom); a short named-account list can justify the trigger event.

**Anti-patterns** (vendor-reported effect): fake Re:/Fwd: (hard fail); pitching the product in the subject (reply drop reported); first name in the subject (fewer replies reported); empty subject (more opens, fewer replies); numbers and percentages (fewer opens reported); stacked punctuation; urgency words ("ASAP", "urgent"); salesy verbs ("boost", "increase", "ROI").

**Contested questions** (vendors contradict each other; hold constant and test, never enforce):
- Casing: title case versus lowercase.
- Question versus statement: a specific pain question can work, a generic one fails; default to statements when unsure.

**Executives** decide in seconds: ultra-concise and understated wins, anything salesy is rejected on sight.

## B2C lifecycle and promotional

Clarity and stated benefit win. Numbers and slightly longer lines, which hurt in B2B cold, are normal here.

**Length**: subject 40 to 60 characters ideal, about 50 or less to survive mobile truncation. Preview text 90 to 140 characters; it extends the subject (completes the thought or adds intrigue) and never repeats it. Subject plus preview text is the tested unit.

**Patterns**: pick the cheapest one the body already supports.
- **Direct**: the event names itself (style: "Your report is ready").
- **Number**: count what the body already lists (style: "3 ways to ...").
- **Question**: name the pain the body answers.
- **How-to**: only if the body genuinely teaches something.
- **Story tease**: needs a real story; intrigue that never pays off costs trust on the next send.

Clear beats clever; specific beats vague. Emoji is polarizing: off by default, a test candidate.

## Parked test queue (each costs sends, not hours)

Run the angle test first: it carries the largest effects. After it, in order: casing, then question versus statement and emoji (tied). Each is one two-arm test at the per-variant floor in `references/test-design.md`. If the list cannot power even one two-arm test, say so and ship the defaults: casing held constant, statements over questions, emoji off.

## Rules for both regimes
- One variable per test, with a written hypothesis before drafting.
- Write for the smallest screen the audience uses.
- Never promise in the subject what the body does not deliver.
- Deceptive subjects are illegal under CAN-SPAM, not just bad practice.
- Variants differ by angle, never by synonym swap.

# E-E-A-T checklist (60 items)

Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness and Trust come from Google's Search Quality Rater Guidelines. They describe what a helpful, trustworthy page looks like. They are not a single ranking score. Trust matters most: an untrustworthy page has low E-E-A-T however expert it looks.

There are 15 items in each of the four areas below.

## Marking rules

Mark every item ✓ (met), ~ (partly met), ✗ (not met) or ? (can't tell).

- ✗ means you looked and it is not there. ? means it cannot be known from what you have. Never use ✗ as a safe guess and never turn a ? into a ✓. Use ? too when an item cannot apply to this subject (for example client pricing in an essay on grief).
- Every ✓ and ~ needs evidence: a short exact quote, a heading or a link. Every ✗ needs "not found".
- Items tagged **[page]** depend on the published page, not just its words: displayed dates, footer links, comments, images, structured data, HTTPS. With a URL, judge them from the fetched page. With pasted text they are ? unless the text itself shows them.
- Items tagged **[site]** need the wider site or the web: About page, reputation, backlinks, the author's other work. Judge them only from lookups you made, and mark the rest ?. If you looked and found nothing where something should be, that is ✗.
- Untagged items are judged from the article text, including the byline, bio, citations and disclosures written into it. For a pasted draft, judge the text as it will be published: no byline in the text is ✗.
- Area score = (✓ + 0.5 × ~) ÷ (items marked ✓, ~ or ✗) × 100, rounded to a whole percent. Example: 2 ✓, 4 ~, 6 ✗ and 3 ? gives (2 + 2) ÷ 12 = 33%. The four counts must add up to 15.
- If fewer than 8 of an area's 15 items are marked ✓, ~ or ✗, report the area as "not scored", leave it out of the composite and say what would unlock it (usually the page URL).
- E-E-A-T composite = the average of the scored areas.

## Length floors by page type

| Page type | Floor (words) |
|---|---|
| Ultimate guide or pillar | 2,500 |
| How-to | 1,200 |
| Listicle or best-of | 1,500 |
| Comparison (X vs Y) | 1,800 |
| Explainer | 1,200 |
| Single-product review | 1,500 |
| Commercial landing page | 600 |

These are rules of thumb, not Google requirements and not targets: a deeply original 1,000-word how-to beats a 2,000-word one padded with intro and conclusion. Use them as a thin-content tripwire. If the article is about a quarter or more below the floor for its type, Experience item 5 and Expertise item 9 are ✗ unless you justify the exception in one line.

## 1. Experience (15 items)

First-hand engagement with the topic. Did the author actually do this?

1. The author has personally used, tested or lived the product, service or process being discussed.
2. Original photos, screenshots or video the author captured. **[page]**
3. Original data the author collected (survey, A/B test, log analysis) is presented.
4. Specific, verifiable details only an experienced practitioner would know (numbers, edge cases, quirks).
5. Step-by-step accounts reflect real execution, not hypothetical steps. (Length floor applies.)
6. Comparative observations across multiple instances ("across 12 client engagements, 8 saw...").
7. Trade-offs and failure modes the author met are acknowledged.
8. Specific dates, places or contexts ground the experience claims.
9. Hands-on demonstrations (live code, working artifacts, before and after evidence).
10. Customer, user or patient quotes attributed to a real source (with permission).
11. Time-based observations ("after 6 months of using X, the result was...").
12. Process improvements or tweaks the author made along the way.
13. Tools, specific configurations or version numbers are mentioned (a sign of real use).
14. Pricing or cost specifics from real engagements, not a price list.
15. Failures or rejected approaches are described, not just successes.

## 2. Expertise (15 items)

The author's qualifications and depth on the topic.

1. Author byline visible with a full name, not "Admin" or a pseudonym.
2. Author bio gives credentials relevant to the topic (degree, certification, professional title).
3. Author bio gives years of experience in the topic.
4. Author has a linkable profile (LinkedIn, professional homepage, ORCID for academics).
5. Author publishes consistently in this topic area (links to other articles). **[site]**
6. Citations to authoritative primary sources (peer-reviewed papers, government data, original research).
7. Industry terminology is used correctly and consistently.
8. Counter-arguments are addressed and engaged, not strawmanned.
9. A methodology section if the piece presents data or analysis. (Length floor applies.)
10. Limitations of the analysis or recommendations are disclosed.
11. Up to date with current developments in the field (recent references, current best practice), judged against today's date.
12. Correlation is distinguished from causation when discussing data.
13. Absolute claims are avoided where the field is genuinely contested.
14. Editorial review credit (for example "reviewed by Dr. X").
15. The author responds to comments or corrections. **[page]**

## 3. Authoritativeness (15 items)

The reputation of the author and the publisher in the topic area. Almost all of these need the wider site or the web, so a pasted draft usually leaves this area "not scored".

1. The publisher has an About page describing its mission, history and team. **[site]**
2. An editorial guidelines or standards page is public. **[site]**
3. The publisher is referenced or cited by other authoritative sources in the field. **[site]**
4. Wikipedia or industry-association mentions of the publisher (a strong signal). **[site]**
5. The author has spoken at industry events, been quoted in trade press or published in authoritative venues. **[site]**
6. Awards or recognition from peer institutions, with verifiable links. **[site]**
7. The publisher's contact information is real and findable (physical address, named team). **[site]**
8. Domain age and consistency: publishing on this topic for years, not weeks. **[site]**
9. Other articles by this author cluster around the same topic. **[site]**
10. Internal links from cornerstone content on this site point to this piece, so it sits in a hub. **[site]**
11. External backlinks to this piece from authoritative sites. **[site]** Use backlink data only if the user named an SEO app and it is connected; otherwise ?.
12. The publisher is a recognised authority for its topic by industry consensus, not just self-description. **[site]**
13. Press or news coverage of the publisher's research or position on this topic. **[site]**
14. Peer publications cite the publisher's earlier work. **[site]**
15. Inclusion in expert lists, "best of" roundups or award lists from outside bodies. **[site]**

## 4. Trustworthiness (15 items)

Signals that the page and its publisher are honest, accurate and accountable.

1. HTTPS with a valid certificate and no mixed content. **[page]** A URL that starts with https:// is ✓ unless something looks wrong; mixed content cannot be checked from text.
2. Privacy policy and terms of service reachable from the page footer. **[page]**
3. The publisher's name, registered business address and a contact route are published. **[site]**
4. Last-updated date visible and recent for the topic's freshness window. **[page]**
5. Original publish date visible. **[page]**
6. Editorial standards or a fact-check policy is linked. **[site]**
7. A corrections or updates log is visible if substantive corrections were made. **[page]**
8. Comment moderation is visible (no spam, signs of engagement). **[page]**
9. Affiliate, sponsored or paid-content disclosures are clear and prominent. Mark ? if nothing is recommended, sold or sponsored.
10. The author bio guards against impersonation (a recognisable photo, social proof links). **[page]**
11. Schema.org `Article` (or a sub-type) with `author` (Person), `publisher` (Organization) and a valid `datePublished`. **[page]** Extracted text rarely shows structured data, so this is usually ?.
12. Site-wide trust signals: customer reviews or testimonials linked to real names or organisations. **[site]**
13. The piece contains no known misinformation about its topic. Check the 2 most specific claims with a lookup: ✓ if they hold, ✗ if one is wrong (say which, and what the source says), ? if you did not check.
14. The sources the piece cites are themselves trustworthy (no Wikipedia-to-Wikipedia chains, no content farms).
15. Pricing, claims and guarantees are clearly stated and not buried.

## AI-writing signs (8)

These do not ban AI use. They flag pieces where nobody seems to have added anything of their own. Mark each one **fired**, **not fired** or **can't tell**, with the evidence. Em dashes alone are never evidence: plenty of human writers use them.

1. **Generic filler phrasing.** Three or more matches from this list: "in today's world", "in today's fast-paced world", "it's important to note that", "let's dive in", "navigating the landscape of", "in conclusion", "when it comes to", "at the end of the day", "delve into", "unlock the power of". Count them and name the ones you found.
2. **The same template across the site.** Five or more recent posts on the same site share an identical heading skeleton and similar length (for example every post is "What is X?", "Benefits of X", "How to use X", "FAQ"). Fired when 4 of 5 match. It needs other posts from the site: can't tell unless you were given them or opened them for another reason.
3. **Nothing first-hand.** No original photo, data, anecdote or specific detail, and every claim is general knowledge you would find on any top result. Fired when Experience scores below 25% and the claims you spot-check are generic.
4. **Em dashes plus bolded phrases.** More than 15 em dashes per 1,000 words and a bolded summary phrase in at least 30% of paragraphs. Both must be present. Borderline is not fired.
5. **Unverifiable citations.** Open up to 3 cited links. Fired if one does not load, the page does not contain the claim, or the "study" cannot be found. If the article cites nothing, mark not fired here: the unsourced-claims check covers that.
6. **No real person behind the byline.** No author named, or a generic byline ("Editorial Team", "Staff Writer", "{Site} Team") with no linked profile, or a profile page with no other articles, no professional profile and no real-world footprint.
7. **No human-review credit.** None of "reviewed by", "fact-checked", "medically reviewed", "edited by" or "last reviewed" anywhere in the article.
8. **Round publishing cadence.** 20 or more articles on unrelated topics within a week, or a brand-new site with 50 or more articles in its first month. It needs the site's post dates: can't tell unless you have them.

Count the fired signs. 4 or more fired, out of those you could judge, means AI-written text is suspected. The count feeds auto-fail 4 below and nothing else; it never lowers a score by itself.

## Auto-fail checks (4)

Any one triggered means NO PUBLISH. Report each one as triggered or not triggered, with evidence.

1. **Anonymous author on a YMYL topic.** The topic is health, money, legal or safety and no author is named (a person's full name, not "Admin" or "Team").
2. **Factual claims with no sources.** The piece asserts statistics, study findings or expert positions about the outside world ("studies show", "experts agree", a percentage) with no inline citation. Trigger when two or more such claims are unsourced, or one the argument depends on is. Claims the author can speak to first-hand (their own product, tests or data) are not unsourced, though they should say when and how they were measured.
3. **Undisclosed affiliate or sponsored relationships.** The piece recommends products or links to them with signs of a commercial relationship (referral or tracking parameters, "use our link", discount codes) or reads as sponsored, and gives no clear disclosure near the recommendation. If no commercial relationship is visible, not triggered. Consumer-protection regulators, for example the US FTC, expect clear disclosure of paid relationships.
4. **AI-written YMYL content with no sign of human review.** 4 or more AI-writing signs fired, and the topic is YMYL, and the article shows none of: a named human editor, a "reviewed by" credit, a "last reviewed" or "fact-checked on" date. AI assistance is fine; AI assistance on health, money, legal or safety content with nobody visibly accountable is not.

## Calibration

- YMYL topics get a higher bar: see the verdict rules in the main instructions.
- For product or commercial content, the Trust items on pricing, claims and disclosure matter most when you rank the fixes.
- Quote the article; do not paraphrase it inside quotation marks.

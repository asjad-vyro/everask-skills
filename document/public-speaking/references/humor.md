# Humor in talks

Use this when the user wants to be funnier, the occasion calls for light humor, or the draft contains jokes. Build every joke from the user's real material. Never invent a story or a fact about a real person.

## The principle (the newsletter post "On being funny at work")

Humor is the strategic assembly of specific words, spoken in a specific way, to create a surprise that produces a smile or a laugh. Set up an expectation, then break it. For any line, ask: where is the surprise, and am I saving it until the right moment? Humor is a learnable skill: study how comedy works, and each time something makes you laugh, work out which technique did it.

## Start with the two zero-risk techniques

- **Specificity.** Swap generic words for specific ones: "Jack Russell terrier" over "dog", "Vienna Fingers" over "cookies". If it gets no laugh, it reads as a clear detail rather than a failed joke.
- **Nostalgia.** Contrast then and now: "We once were this. Now we are this." / "The world once looked like this. Thanks to us, it now looks like this." If it gets no laugh, it reads as useful history. Use it to show how far a product or team has come.

## The seven strategies (from the post)

1. **Nostalgia:** as above.
2. **Exaggeration:** an overstatement so outrageous nobody thinks you are lying ("a VCR that weighed about 750 pounds"). The post's tip: words that start with a hard K tend to be funnier ("kabillion").
3. **One of these things is not like the other:** two normal items, then a surprising third. Template: [normal], [normal] and [surprising, emotional or absurd]. Example: "canned pineapples, ham, and despair." A business version: "simplify workflows, shorten the sales cycle, and bore your family to death when you tell them about it tonight."
4. **Definitions:** reduce something to its single most amusing defining attribute, as if sifting it through a magical colander. The serious version is "1,000 songs in your pocket". It works well on competitors.
5. **Specificity:** as above.
6. **Stating the obvious:** walk through a space or a process in your head and note what is odd, arcane, inexplicable or less than ideal about each thing. Those are your openings.
7. **The unexpected:** put the funniest word or idea last.

## Landing a line (Dharmesh Shah)

1. Put the funny words at the very end of the sentence, even if that makes the phrasing slightly awkward.
2. Stop talking right after it.
3. Wait half a second to give the audience permission to laugh.
4. Stack several punchlines on a single setup.

Measure with laughs per minute (LPM), the stand-up metric: record the talk, mark where people actually laughed, and either shorten or add a joke to long stretches with no reaction.

## Funniest word last (Matthew Dicks)

Identify the funniest part of the moment, then say it last. Resist leading with the best bit; change the order of events if that protects the surprise.

- Weaker: "I dreamed last night that I was playing the new Minecraft update."
- Stronger: "Dad! I got the new Minecraft update. I played it a little. Then I woke up."

## Callbacks

Introduce a specific detail or character early, then bring it back one to three times in new contexts. Each return gets a laugh because the audience feels in on it.

## Risk (Sam Lessin)

- A joke nobody laughs at is a real social cost. Self-deprecating humor is the safe default.
- Teasing other people needs deep familiarity and is high risk. Do not joke at the expense of someone in the room unless the user knows they will enjoy it.
- Keep a few ready stories ranked by how safe they are for different rooms. Do not become known only as the comedian.

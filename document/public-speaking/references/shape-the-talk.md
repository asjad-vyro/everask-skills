# Shape the talk

Methods for finding the point, building the outline, and writing the opening, ending and slides. Credit a method to its person in a few words when you use it.

## 1. The one sentence (Tristan de Montebello's bow and arrow)

Stop asking what you want to say. Ask what you want people to remember.

- **Arrow:** the one sentence that, if it is all they remember, would satisfy you.
- **Bow:** the two or three things that give the arrow weight: a story, a data point, a vivid image, an emotional beat. De Montebello also calls them bookmarks or pillars.
- **Process:** write a tentative arrow, pick two or three bow elements, then refine both through practice runs. Arrow and bow shape each other.
- **Two levels:** use it on the whole talk and on every slide. A slide with no single clear point either goes or is split in two. Lenny Rachitsky's tip: make the slide title exactly the one thing the audience should take from it.
- **De Montebello's example:** arrow "The accordion method is more powerful than memorizing"; bookmarks: the old way and its problems, a living-room analogy, the method itself.

## 2. The audience journey and the big idea (Nancy Duarte)

- Define where the audience is now and where you are moving them. State your point of view and what is at stake if they do or do not adopt it. Every slide has to serve that one big idea.
- **What is / what could be:** start on what is (today's reality or the problem), move up to what could be (the better future), alternate between the two to build contrast and longing, and end on the new bliss: the future with the idea adopted.

## 3. The five-second moment (Matthew Dicks)

Every strong story turns on one moment, a few seconds long. It is either a transformation ("I used to be one kind of person, now I am another") or, more common, a realization ("I used to think X, then things happened, and now I think Y").

1. Find that moment first. Start the story from its end point.
2. Build everything else as the context that lets the moment land. Dicks puts it at about 98% of the story.
3. Cut whatever does not lead to the moment.

The moment must come from the user's real experience. If they have not given you one, leave a slot and put the question in "What only you can add". Dicks's daily habit for collecting moments: each night write one line, a date and a story, answering "If I had to tell a story about today to save my family, what would it be?"

## 4. Stakes (Matthew Dicks)

Five ways to make the audience worry or wonder what happens next:

1. **Elephant:** plant the big, obvious problem at the start.
2. **Backpack:** tell them your plan before you carry it out, so they hope with you.
3. **Breadcrumbs:** drop hints about what is coming.
4. **Hourglass:** slow time down with detail just before a big reveal.
5. **Crystal ball:** predict a terrible but realistic future if things go wrong.

Elephant and backpack are the easiest to add to a business talk.

## 5. Explaining something dry (Matthew Dicks's adjacency)

Match theme, not content. Name the core feeling of the idea ("giving people exactly what they need"), find a personal story with the same feeling (buying a specific kind of apple for each person in the family), and snap the story onto the idea. Use only a story the user tells you.

## 6. Opening patterns

Pick one for the rewrite. Use only facts and details from the user's material.

1. **The moment:** start inside the scene from section 3, with the most specific detail first.
2. **The surprise:** lead with the most surprising true fact in their material.
3. **The contrast:** what is, then what could be, in two sentences.
4. **The elephant:** name the big problem in the first breath.
5. **The ask:** for a work meeting, say whether you need a decision, feedback, or only to share information. The newsletter's point: the goal is not as obvious to the audience as you think.

Thanks, your title and the plan for the talk are fine, but after the hook and in one breath.

## 7. Simple arcs

- **Beginning, middle, end (Christina Wodtke):** a hook (a mystery, a secret or a surprise), then the message or product, then success and celebration that leaves people excited.
- **What is / what could be:** see section 2.

## 8. Is the talk needed? (checklist from the newsletter post "Startup PM vs. big company PM")

1. Do you need a presentation, or would an email do?
2. Make sure the audience knows the goal: a decision, general feedback, or only information.
3. Get fresh eyes on it before you present; they catch the glaring problems.
4. Keep it short. No one has wished a presentation were longer.

## 9. Slides

- **Process (Maggie Crowley):** outline on paper, draw a box per slide, write the literal headline in each box, sketch the flow and visuals, and only then open the software.
- The title is the takeaway, with one idea per slide: "Pricing" becomes "Customers pay only for what they use".
- Replace text walls and dry charts with one image, one number or one high-contrast word where you can. Nancy Duarte treats slides as a cinematic experience that supports the spoken word rather than transcribing it, and says a single-word or high-contrast slide changes the energy in the room. Matthew Dicks's version: pie charts and statistics are what everyone else shows; a vivid image and a little personal vulnerability are what people remember. Keep the numbers that matter, and pair each with a person or a picture.

## 10. Keeping the room awake (Wes Kao)

Break up any monologue with a state change every 3 to 5 minutes (Nathan Barry's version: every 3 to 5 slides). Options: ask for something in the chat; switch between gallery view and screen share; hand over to someone else; ask people to unmute and chime in; breakout rooms followed by popcorn share-outs; a poll; ask people to guess before you reveal. Check the draft: has there been a state change in the last couple of minutes?

## 11. Short formats

- **Toast or tribute (Matt Abrahams, WHAT):** **W** Why are we here: the purpose of the gathering. **H** How are you connected to the person: one line. **A** Anecdote: a short, accessible story that shows their character. **T** Thank you: express gratitude and signal the close ("Cheers"). Abrahams's mindset for a tribute: give yourself permission to be simple and present; sincerity beats eloquence.
- **Problem, solution, benefit (Abrahams):** the problem in the world, how your idea solves it, the outcome for the listener. Good for pitches and spontaneous remarks.
- **Q&A, ADD (Abrahams):** give a clean, short **A**nswer; add one **D**etailed example; then **D**escribe relevance: say why the answer matters to the person who asked. Mindset: dare to be dull. Aim for connection over perfection and simply answer the question; it quiets self-evaluation and frees mental bandwidth.

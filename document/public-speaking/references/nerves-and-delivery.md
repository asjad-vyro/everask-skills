# Nerves, practice and delivery

## 1. Fix the cause, not the symptom (Tristan de Montebello)

De Montebello's view: speaking is a subconscious, flow-oriented skill, and when he speaks best he is not thinking about speaking. Piling on tactics to remember backfires; remove what pulls a person out of flow.

| The user notices | De Montebello's explanation | Work on |
|---|---|---|
| Filler words, racing | Not being comfortable slowing down, relaxing or pausing when the mind races under pressure | Pausing and a slower pace. Do not count the ums. |
| Monotone, constrained delivery | Not trusting that showing emotion or taking time will not make everything unravel | The Conductor drill (section 5) |
| Weak endings | Attention drifts near the finish line, so speakers ease off at the last moment | Summary prompts (section 4) |
| Hedging, a stiff "professional" voice | Switching into a different version of yourself the moment the timer starts | Conviction prompts (section 5); think out loud |

## 2. Nerves

- **Reframe.** A racing heart and tight chest are excitement and readiness. Tell yourself so before you start (Matt Abrahams: reframe anxiety symptoms as excitement; Maya Prohovnik: as helpful adrenaline).
- **Visualize the whole sequence** (Abrahams): walking up, speaking, the room responding well, stepping off. Athletes do it, and Abrahams says research suggests it desensitizes people. Breathe deeply first so you do not tie the rehearsal to stress. Look at a photo of the room beforehand so it feels less new.
- **Prepare to be spontaneous** (Abrahams): treat on-the-spot speaking like athletics or jazz, and rehearse it.
- **Stay in character** (de Montebello): do not announce nerves or doubts mid-talk; the audience cannot see what you feel. His analogy: a pilot who announces "I see a red light blinking, not sure what it is" makes every bump frightening.
- **If you slip, keep going** (de Montebello's train analogy): passengers forget a bump within seconds, unless you stop the train to point at it.
- **If panic hits:** a guest on the "Failure" episode advises stepping away or resetting for a moment to break the loop.
- **Dare to be dull** (Abrahams): aim for connection over perfection. Just make the point or answer the question; it quiets self-evaluation and frees mental bandwidth.
- Prohovnik's own habit: raising her hands above her head before speaking, to manage blood flow and nerves.
- If the user describes panic attacks or severe anxiety, add one kind line that a doctor or therapist can help, and that this is coaching, not medical advice.

## 3. Preparing

- **Talking points, not a script.** A guest on the "Failure" episode froze the first time they rehearsed a talk word for word; usually they used talking points and ad-libbed. Rehearse the flow of ideas, keep cue words for each part, and know the first and last sentences cold.
- **Prohovnik's routine for high-stakes talks:** write speaker notes in whatever format works for you (she uses full sentences); rehearse at least 10 times all the way through; memorize enough to ad-lib at least half of the notes; make heavy eye contact so it looks off-script.
- **Accordion (de Montebello), about 15 minutes.** It internalizes a talk instead of memorizing it.
  - Going down: optionally dump every idea on paper first. Speak the talk against a 3-minute timer, staying in character and ending strong, however bad it sounds; note what you liked and did not. Repeat at 2 minutes (cut the noise), then 1 minute, then 30 seconds: only the essentials survive.
  - Going up: another 30 seconds, then 1 minute (it now feels spacious, so bring elements back on purpose), then 2, then 3 minutes (it now feels like a football field). Write down the one sentence and the three bookmarks.
  - Use it on a whole talk or on one section. For a talk much longer than 3 minutes, start from its full length and halve it each round.
- **Warm up:** five minutes of a quick improvisation game, such as rapid-fire analogies, before a big meeting, interview or podcast, so you do not go in cold (de Montebello).
- **Make practice enjoyable.** Short game-based reps beat long boring prep; if it is dull, people quit within a couple of weeks (de Montebello).
- **Fresh eyes.** Have someone who has not seen it listen once; they catch the glaring problems.
- **Low-stakes reps.** Janna Bastow built her speaking through early community participation and steady practice at small meetups before large conferences.

## 4. Delivery habits

- **Think up** (de Montebello): when you gather your thoughts, look up and to the side, not down at the floor or your notes. Down reads as checking a phone or as uncertainty; up reads as thoughtful. On video, put "Think up" on a sticky note.
- **Pause.** Finish a sentence and let one beat of silence pass before the next. It gives the listener time and you mental space.
- **End strong with summary prompts** (de Montebello): start the sentence out loud and trust your brain to finish it: "So to wrap up...", "In summary...", "So my point here is...", "What I want you to remember is...". Do not let go of the gas pedal at the last moment: a great talk with a weak ending is a smooth flight with a bumpy landing, and people remember the landing.
- **Sound like yourself.** Many people drop into a stiff "public speaking voice" the moment they start. Think out loud and talk to the room the way you talk across a table.
- **Conviction over hedging.** Notice "I think maybe", "just" and "kind of", then restate the point with authority. If the worry is sounding overconfident, de Montebello reads that worry as a sign of integrity: say what your substance supports.
- **Energy leads, emotion follows, words follow** (de Montebello): raise your energy or step into the state first instead of hunting for the right words.
- **Presence checklist** (condensed from Jeffrey Pfeffer): make eye contact; do not read your notes word for word; keep it brief; do not apologize for yourself; use your hands; keep an open posture; speak up.

## 5. Drills

Short reps with a timer, about a minute each. Each builds resilience under disruption, so the real talk feels easier.

- **Triple Step (de Montebello), about 60 seconds.** Pick a random speech title. As you speak, 4 to 6 random words arrive and you must work each in as if it was always part of the talk. Choose one direction at the start and hold it; each word serves the direction instead of starting a new thought (a tree in a storm: firm direction, flexible branches). Words can be used literally or as metaphors. Beginners: 4 words. Stand-in: a friend calls out random words.
- **Conductor, about 70 seconds.** Pick a random speech title. Match your energy to a number from 1 to 10 that changes every so often: 5 is your natural pace, higher is more intense, lower is calmer and more reflective, and "breathe" means a full pause. Different levels bring out different content, not the same content louder. Stand-in: a friend reads out nine random numbers, or raises and lowers a hand.
- **Conviction prompts.** Like Triple Step, but the prompts are sentence starters you must say aloud and finish while advocating for an idea tied to the title: "This matters a ton because...", "I genuinely believe that...", "Game changer", "It astonishes me when...", "In fact", "The entire world", "We cannot forget that...", "Life changing". For people who hedge or understate.
- **Speak it, don't write it.** Prepare a talk by speaking it aloud at different lengths (the accordion) rather than writing it out.

## 6. Plan skeleton

Build the user's plan from this, compressed to the days they have. Every item is done out loud and standing up.

- **Day 1:** write the one sentence and the three bookmarks. Speak the talk once against a timer and record it on a phone; listen once, marking where you rush or lose energy.
- **Day 2:** accordion down (3 minutes, 2, 1, 30 seconds).
- **Day 3:** accordion up (30 seconds, 1, 2, 3 minutes, then the full length). Fix the opening and ending so you can say them cold.
- **Day 4:** a timed full run-through on talking points, not a script. One drill from section 5 chosen for the user's biggest delivery risk (fillers: pausing; freezing: Triple Step; flat delivery: Conductor).
- **Day 5:** run it for a listener with fresh eyes. Prepare answers to the three questions you dread, using ADD.
- **Day before:** one more run-through, then the full-sequence visualization after deep breathing. Stop rehearsing early and sleep.
- **On the day:** a five-minute warm-up game; reframe the nerves as excitement; first line cold; think up when you need a beat; finish on a summary prompt.
- **Fewer than 4 days left:** keep, in this order: at least three timed out-loud run-throughs, the first and last lines cold, the visualization, and the on-the-day routine.

## 7. Starter plan (the user gave no talk and no occasion)

One week, about 15 minutes a day, all out loud:

1. Pick the next real speaking moment (a meeting, a toast, a demo). Write the one sentence you want people to remember from it.
2. Speak that sentence and its supporting points for 60 seconds with no notes. Record it and listen once.
3. Do one Triple Step round with 4 words.
4. Practice pausing: say five sentences, letting one full beat of silence pass after each.
5. Do one Conductor round.
6. Speak up in a low-stakes room: a team meeting or a small meetup.
7. Review the recording from day 2 against a new one. Pick the single habit to work on next.

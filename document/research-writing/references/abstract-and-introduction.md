# Abstracts and introductions

## The abstract

Default order. Follow the journal's structured format (for example Background, Methods, Results, Conclusions) when it requires one, and keep to its word limit.

1. **Problem and stakes**: what the problem is and who or what it affects, in one or two specific sentences. Use only the problem and stakes the user gave. If they gave none, open with the study's own question in neutral words ("This trial tested whether...") instead of asserting why the topic matters.
2. **Gap**: what is unknown or unsolved, and why current approaches fall short, as the user described it. If the user did not say why current practice falls short, leave the gap implicit in the question, or put `[why current practice falls short]` where it belongs. Do not make up a rationale.
3. **What was done**: design, data or sample, and method. Name them and give the numbers the user gave.
4. **Key results**: the one to three findings that carry the paper, with the numbers and the comparison. If the user's material states no result, leave a placeholder and list it in the reply. Never invent one.
5. **Meaning**: what the result changes, limited to what was tested, with the main limitation in a clause if there is room. Keep any "what next" as general as the user's own ("further research is needed") or tie it to a limitation the user stated; add no recommendation for practice or policy that the user did not make.

Check every abstract against this list:

- Could it belong to any paper in the field? Then it is too vague: name the method, the data and the comparison.
- Does it state a result? "We investigated whether..." on its own is a plan, not an abstract.
- Does every number carry its unit, its sample and what it is compared with?
- Is every claim limited to the sample and setting that were tested ("in this sample", "at one site")? Remove "useful in many settings" unless the user's evidence covers many.
- If the user's own numbers do not support the user's wording (for example "much faster" beside a 3% gain), report the numbers and fix the wording.
- Does it stand alone: no undefined abbreviations, no "see Figure 2", no citations unless the venue expects them?
- Tense: past for what was done and found; present for what the paper argues or what holds generally.
- No filler opener ("X is important", "In this paper, we").
- Is it within the limit, counted with code?

## The introduction

Default sequence of six moves. Reorder or skip only when the field or venue differs (see the notes below).

| # | Move | What it does | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Stakes | Says why the problem matters, naming the people, uses or scale involved (only what the user gave you). Opens with the problem, not the tool. | Would an expert in the target field care about the first sentence? |
| 2 | Gap | Names the limit of current approaches: they assume X, which fails when Y. Number the limits if there are several. Name the approaches. A structural gap ("cannot do X because...") is stronger than a quantitative one ("not good enough"). | Are the existing approaches named, not just "prior work"? |
| 3 | Core idea | States the contribution in one sentence. Give the idea a short name only if it genuinely has one; do not invent a brand name. | Is this what a reader would remember after a minute's skim? |
| 4 | Approach in one paragraph | The mental model: how it works at the highest level, as a pipeline or a design, with no detail. | Can a reader follow the approach from this paragraph alone? |
| 5 | Contributions | Numbered or bulleted. Each is a claim ("We show that...") with the section that supports it, not an activity ("We propose..."). | Does each contribution have evidence you can point to? If not, it is an aspiration: cut it or soften it. |
| 6 | Results preview | The headline result or two, with numbers the user gave. Leave it blank if there are none yet. | Do the numbers match the results section exactly? |

Rules that matter most:

- **The introduction promises exactly what the paper delivers.** After the results are final, check every promise in the introduction against a result. Remove or soften any promise that has none.
- **Results not final yet?** Write an early version that sets out the argument and the evidence still to collect, with move 6 left blank. When the results are in, rewrite it from the final evidence instead of editing it.
- **Problem first, method second.** The method should not appear before the reader knows what problem it solves.
- **Not every field fits.** In theory or humanities writing use: the question or puzzle, what the existing debate says, the claim (the thesis), a roadmap. Keep stakes, gap and claim; drop the results preview.
- **Common failures**: opening with the technology ("X has shown great promise"); vague stakes; a gap that is only "more work is needed"; contributions written as activities; promising more than the evidence shows.

Self-check: if you kept only the first sentence of each paragraph, would the argument still read? Does the order of the contributions match the order of the sections that follow?

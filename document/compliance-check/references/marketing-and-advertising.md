# Marketing, advertising and consumer protection quick reference

Starting points for actions that involve contacting people, advertising, promotions or claims about a product. These are pointers, not a full statement of the law: confirm the rules you rely on (Step 3), and check how they apply in the country of the people being reached, not only where the company is based.

## Marketing emails, texts and calls

| Where | Rule | What it asks |
|---|---|---|
| EU/EEA | ePrivacy Directive (Article 13), implemented in each country's national law, alongside GDPR | Prior opt-in consent for marketing emails and texts to individuals, to the GDPR standard (no pre-ticked boxes, provable, easy to withdraw). Exception ("soft opt-in"): a company may email its own customers about its own similar products or services if it got their address during a sale and offered a simple, free way to object at collection and in every message. People who have never bought cannot be reached this way. Every message must identify the sender and carry a working opt-out. Countries differ in detail; Germany, for example, expects consent you can prove, so double opt-in is the usual practice. |
| UK | PECR, alongside UK GDPR | Same opt-in rule and soft opt-in as the EU. The ICO enforces. |
| US | CAN-SPAM Act (email); TCPA and state laws (texts and calls) | Email needs no opt-in, but: no misleading headers or subject lines, identify the message as an ad, include a physical postal address, include a clear opt-out and honor it within 10 business days. Marketing texts and autodialed or prerecorded calls generally need prior express written consent; check Do-Not-Call rules and state laws. |
| Canada | CASL | Express or implied consent, sender identification and a working unsubscribe. |
| Australia | Spam Act 2003 | Consent, sender identification and an unsubscribe facility. |

Also check how the list was built (bought or scraped lists are a red flag), what people were told at sign-up, and whether the sign-up consent covers the new purpose.

## Ads, testimonials, reviews and influencers

| Where | Rule | What it asks |
|---|---|---|
| US | FTC Act section 5; FTC Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255); FTC rule on consumer reviews and testimonials (2024); state right-of-publicity laws | Endorsements must reflect the endorser's honest experience. Any material connection (payment, free product, discount, affiliate commission, employment, family tie) must be clearly and conspicuously disclosed in the ad itself. The business answers for what its endorsers say. A testimonial that implies typical results needs evidence that those results are typical; "results not typical" fine print is not enough. Fake or paid-for reviews, hidden insider reviews and review suppression are prohibited. Using a person's name, photo or words in an ad needs their permission; keep it in writing. |
| UK | CAP Code (enforced by the ASA); CMA consumer-protection rules | Ads must be obviously identifiable as ads. Testimonials must be genuine, documented and used with the giver's permission. Paid or gifted content must be labeled. |
| EU | Unfair Commercial Practices Directive, as updated by the Omnibus Directive (EU) 2019/2161; national advertising codes | Hidden advertising and fake reviews are banned; sponsored content must be disclosed; reviews presented as genuine must come from real customers. |
| Platforms | Instagram and Meta advertising and branded-content policies | Use the platform's paid-partnership label where it applies, and check the current ad policies. |

Privacy overlay: using a customer's name, photo or words is processing personal data, so in the EU and UK a lawful basis (usually consent) and a privacy notice are needed as well. A customer's text or photo may also be their copyright, so permission should cover that use too.

## Promotions, discounts, prize draws and referral rewards

- **Price claims** ("was/now", "50% off"): the reference price must be real. In the EU a price reduction must be measured against the lowest price of the previous 30 days; US pricing guides, state laws and UK rules also catch fake "original" prices.
- **Prize draws and giveaways**: a draw where people pay or buy to enter and winners are picked by chance can be an illegal lottery. Many places also require published official rules and a free way to enter, and some US states require registration for large prizes.
- **Referral and cash rewards**: describe the offer fully (terms, eligibility, limits, expiry). Rewards tied to reviews or endorsements must be disclosed. Check tax reporting for cash payments to individuals and sector rules (financial products, insurance, healthcare and real estate restrict referral payments). Rewards for recruiting rather than for real sales can be treated as a pyramid scheme. A message sent to a referred friend on the company's behalf can count as marketing to someone who never opted in.
- **Claims about a product** (health, earnings, performance, environmental): the evidence must be in hand before the ad runs.

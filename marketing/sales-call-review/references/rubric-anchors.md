# Rubric anchors

Anchors are written at 0, 2 and 4; interpolate 1 and 3. Score nothing without a quote. The phrases quoted below as examples of behaviour are generic illustrations, not lines from the user's call.

## Opener: cold call variant

Goal: earn explicit opt-in and an environment where a real conversation can happen, which earns more time.

| Anchor | What it sounds like on tape |
|---|---|
| 0 | Opens with a known conversation-stopper ("I was calling about our solution", "I wanted to see if you're interested in exploring our platform", "Have you heard of us before?", "How's your day going?") or launches into a pitch; the prospect never opts in |
| 2 | A permission-based or relevance-led open is attempted, but the opt-in is grudging or unclear, the tone reads scripted or apologetic, or the earned time is spent on product talk instead of the prospect's problem |
| 4 | Explicit opt-in earned quickly; the open leads with the prospect's world (a problem, a trigger, a relevant observation), not the product; the rep is direct and warm in the words used |

Evidence cues: the prospect's opt-in words or their absence; the first thing the rep says after opt-in; any conversation-stopper phrase.

## Opener: scheduled meeting variant (agenda set)

Framework: Purpose, Plan, Outcome.

| Anchor | What it sounds like on tape |
|---|---|
| 0 | No agenda at all: the call drifts into small talk or a demo with no stated purpose or destination |
| 2 | Partial agenda: purpose stated but no plan for the time or no proposed outcome, and no check for the prospect's own agenda |
| 4 | Purpose, plan and outcome all stated, closed with an open check on the prospect's goals ("is there anything else you wanted to get out of this one?") |

Evidence cues: the first two minutes; whether a decision or next-step outcome was proposed up front; the prospect's reply to the agenda check.

## Discovery

Method: problem-first questioning, in the tradition of SPIN-style questioning (situation, problem, implication, need-payoff), where better performers in complex sales are commonly described as asking more problem and implication questions than situation questions. Treat that as a commonly quoted heritage, not a measured result for this rep. An interrogation checklist ("death by a thousand questions") is a failure, not a win.

| Anchor | What it sounds like on tape |
|---|---|
| 0 | Interrogation checklist (rapid generic questions, no follow-through on answers), or no discovery at all and straight to pitch; the prospect's answers shrink over the call |
| 2 | Real open questions, but the rep stays at the surface symptom and never ladders to consequence or cost, or drills deep into a problem without ever testing whether it matters to the prospect |
| 4 | Problem-first: questions build on the prospect's answers, ladder from symptom toward business consequence, and explicitly test priority ("is this worth solving?") before going deep; collaborative "we" framing; the prospect does most of the talking |

Evidence cues: whether follow-up questions quote or build on the prospect's previous answer; any priority-test question (for example "if there is one thing it needed to do better, what comes to mind?"); the longest prospect monologue and what prompted it. A talk-to-listen ratio is a vendor claim when it comes from a conversation-intelligence platform's own data: correlational, context only, never an anchor.

## Objection handling

Method: agree, disarm, redirect; or listen, restate, resolve, confirm. The objection is not counted as handled until the prospect confirms aloud.

If no objection surfaced: note whether strong opener and discovery seem to have prevented it or the prospect was simply disengaged, then mark N/A.

| Anchor | What it sounds like on tape |
|---|---|
| 0 | Argues with or talks over the objection, capitulates instantly (a discount, "I'll send info"), or ignores it; the objection is never restated |
| 2 | A rebuttal is delivered, but the objection was never restated back, or resolution was never confirmed aloud: the rep moved on assuming it landed |
| 4 | Objection acknowledged without defensiveness, restated in the prospect's words, addressed or consciously parked, and the prospect verbally confirms resolution before the call moves on |

Evidence cues: the objection verbatim; the rep's first sentence after it; any restatement; the prospect's confirmation words or their absence.

## Close (next-step close, not contract signature)

Three checks:

- Continued-investment commitment: does the prospect say they want to move forward?
- Timeline pressure-tested: does the rep ask when the prospect wants to buy or decide, and flag honestly if the date is distant? A date far out signals low priority.
- The rep, not the prospect, proposes concrete immediate and follow-up steps.

Quality ladder: never setting steps is bad; setting steps on every call regardless of deal reality is mediocre; setting steps on real deals only is good. A calendared step on a dead deal is theatre, not a close.

| Anchor | What it sounds like on tape |
|---|---|
| 0 | No next step proposed, or the call ends on "send me some info" with nothing scheduled and no pushback |
| 2 | A next step is set but hollow: no investment check, no timeline test, no owner or date; or a step is forced onto a call where the deal is visibly not real |
| 4 | Investment commitment sought, timeline pressure-tested (low priority flagged honestly if the answer is distant), and the rep proposes specific immediate and follow-up steps with owner, date and purpose |

Evidence cues: the timeline question and the buyer's answer; who proposed the step; whether date, owner and purpose were all stated; whether the step matches the deal's apparent reality.

## Optional B2C or contact-centre dimension: compliance and script adherence

Used only for B2C, inside-sales or contact-centre calls. Score as pass or fail items, not 0 to 4. This is an adaptation of contact-centre QA practice, not an established sales-call rubric: say so in the review's Notes. Fit the items to the user's house script and compliance list when given; if not given, use these generic items and label them "assumed".

- Required disclosures delivered (recording notice, terms, cancellation or cooling-off rights where applicable): pass or fail each.
- Claims accurate: no price, benefit or availability statement that contradicts the product's actual terms as the user gave them. If the terms were not given, flag the claim as "unverified" rather than failing it.
- Script checkpoints hit where the house script mandates them. Still penalize robotic verbatim reading under the opener and discovery anchors; adherence and conversation quality are scored separately.

Any compliance failure is a fatal moment and becomes the focus behaviour, whatever its effort to land, because a recorded misstatement cannot be unsaid.

## Good and bad review, for calibration

A bad review: "Great call overall, 9/10, meeting booked so clearly it worked. Strengths: awesome energy, likeable, great rapport. Improve: be more consultative, better discovery, stronger close, more urgency, set next steps on every call." It grades the outcome, quotes nothing, uses character language, lists far more than three items with no ranking, and coaches next-steps theatre. Rebuild such a review from the transcript rather than editing it.

A good review quotes the exact lines, finds the one moment that mattered most, ranks few items, and ends with one action the rep can repeat on the next call and the evidence that would show it landed.

# Adapting the message across channels

Same story, different telling. The core stays constant: the customer is the hero, the business is the guide, the problem is named at three levels, there is a plan, and the stakes are clear. What changes is the length and format, the tone, the platform's habits and the entry point into the story.

Rules:
- Use only facts the user gave you. Write a bracketed slot for any result, quote or number you don't have. Never invent an anecdote ("Last week a client told me..." only if the user supplied it).
- Everything is a draft for the user to review. Don't post or send anything.

## Social

**LinkedIn** (professional, short on time, wants career or business value). Lead with the problem or the outcome, use short paragraphs, keep a human professional tone, end with a clear call to action or a question.
- One-liner post: "Most [customers] struggle with [problem]. Here's the simple fix: [plan in 3 bullets]. The result: [success]. [Call to action]."
- Story post: only with a real story the user gave: the situation, what was wrong, what changed, the result, a transitional call to action.

**X (Twitter)** (fast, opinionated, values brevity). One idea per tweet, with a strong hook first.
- Single: "[Problem] -> [solution] -> [result]. [Call to action]"
- Thread: 1 hook (problem or bold claim); 2 to 4 the explanation; 5 the plan; 6 what's possible (stakes); 7 call to action.

**Instagram** (visual first, values authenticity). The first line is the headline. Caption: hook, relatable problem, what worked, the outcome, call to action (save, share, link in bio), a few relevant hashtags. Hooks: "POV: you're struggling with [problem]", "3 things I wish I knew about [topic]". Carousels suit teaching; stories suit behind-the-scenes proof that the business is a credible guide.

**Facebook** (broader audience, community). Longer, conversational posts are fine. Ask questions to start comments; use native video; groups and live Q&A build authority around the transformation.

**TikTok and short video** (entertainment first, raw beats polished). Hooks: "Nobody tells you this about [topic]", "Here's why [common approach] doesn't work". Structure: 0 to 3 seconds hook; 3 to 15 the problem or tension; 15 to 45 the solution; 45 to 60 call to action or punchline.

## Video and webinars

- **YouTube:** intro (the problem, what the viewer will learn, a reason to stay); problem section (external, internal, why common fixes fail); solution section (the plan, examples, real proof); stakes (what's possible, what happens if nothing changes); call to action.
- **Webinar:** opening (the promise, why listen, ground rules); content (problem deep-dive, the approach, real cases, implementation steps); transition ("Want help implementing this?"); the offer (what's included, who it's for, price, guarantee if real); close (FAQ, final call to action; scarcity only if genuine).
- **Testimonial video, questions for customers:** What was your situation before? What was the hardest part? Why did you choose us? What was it like working with us? What results have you seen? What would you tell someone considering it? Edit in that order: problem, why they chose, the experience, the results, the recommendation.

## Podcasts

- **As a guest:** intro pitch ("I help [customers] with [problem] so they can [outcome]. Most people try [common mistake], but what works is [approach]"); have ready a short origin story (stay the guide, not the hero), a real client transformation and a contrarian take; agree one call to action ("If you want [resource], go to [URL]").
- **Own show:** hook, the problem and what's wrong with current thinking, the approach, a real example, what the listener should do next, call to action. For interviews: why this guest matters to the audience's problem, their struggle, what they figured out, tactical advice, where to learn more.

## PR and media

- **Press release:** an outcome-focused headline, not "[Company] launches new product" but "[Customers] can now [outcome] with [product]". Open with who is helped, what problem is solved, the key benefit. Then the guide's credentials, how it works, real early results or quotes, why it matters now. The founder quote talks about customer transformation, not company pride. The boilerplate is the one-liner, not a feature list.
- **Interview prep:** frame the problem (external and internal), summarise the plan, line up real proof points, and prepare one sentence on the problem, one on the solution, one real sourced statistic if the user has one, and a 30-second real customer story. Bridges: "The real issue is...", "What matters most to [customers] is...".

## Newsletter

Weekly shape: subject (benefit plus curiosity); hook (a problem or observation); the main story or insight; the takeaway; one call to action; an optional P.S.

| Type | Purpose | Script focus |
|------|---------|--------------|
| Educational | Build authority | The guide shows expertise |
| Story | Build connection | The customer's journey |
| Promotional | Drive action | Clear call to action and stakes |
| Update | Keep the relationship | The guide stays present |

## Keeping it consistent

- **Voice:** professional but approachable, confident but not arrogant, empathetic but not condescending. List the words to use and to avoid, and the phrases that put the customer in the hero seat.
- **Before publishing anything:** Is the customer the hero? Is the business the guide? Is the problem addressed (external and internal)? Is there a clear path? Are success and failure shown? Is there one clear call to action? Does the tone match? Does the format suit the platform?
- **Master message document:** keep the core message (the one-liner), the problem statements, the guide's credentials, the plan, the success and failure pictures, and both calls to action in one place, and write every channel from it.

# Lyric craft

Use what helps the song and drop what doesn't. Check your draft against this before you send it.

## Structures

```
ABABCB  Verse / Chorus / Verse / Chorus / Bridge / Chorus   (most pop and rock)
AABA    Verse / Verse / Bridge / Verse                      (ballads, standards)
ABAB    Verse / Chorus alternating                          (simple, direct)
AAA     Verse / Verse / Verse, no chorus                    (folk, storytelling; end each verse on the same refrain line)
```

The building blocks:

- Intro: sets the mood and pulls the listener in.
- Verse: the story, the details, the world.
- Pre-Chorus: an optional ramp of tension before the payoff.
- Chorus: the emotional core, the part people remember.
- Bridge: a detour, a shift of perspective.
- Outro: the farewell. It can echo the rest or turn it.

Use only the blocks the length allows. Structure serves the emotion, not the other way around.

## Rhyme and meter

Rhyme types, from tight to loose:

- Perfect: lean / mean
- Family: crate / braid
- Assonance: had / glass (same vowels, different endings)
- Consonance: scene / when (different vowels, similar endings)
- Near or slant: enough to suggest a connection without locking it down

Mix them. All perfect rhymes can sound like a nursery rhyme; all slant rhymes can sound lazy.

Internal rhyme, inside a line and not just at its end, adds energy: "I took the long road home, alone, where the river bends."

Meter is the rhythm of stressed and unstressed syllables:

- Parallel lines with the same number of stressed syllables are easy to sing. The stressed syllables matter more than the total count.
- Say each line out loud. If you stumble, fix the meter.
- Break the meter on purpose only when it adds emphasis or a surprise.

## Energy and contrast

A song is a journey, not a flat road. Rough energy out of 10: Intro 2 to 3, Verse 5 to 6, Pre-Chorus 7, Chorus 8 to 9, Final Chorus 9 to 10, Bridge varies.

Contrast is the strongest tool: sparse before dense, low before high, a whisper before a roar. The drop only works because of the build. "Whisper to roar to whisper" suits ballads, epics and anthems: start intimate, build to full power, strip back to something fragile. Say this arc in the style prompt too.

## Lines that sing

- Show, don't tell. "I was sad" is flat; "Your hoodie's still on the hook by the door" is alive. Sometimes a plain line said straight is the power, so do that rarely and on purpose.
- The hook is the line people hum and repeat, usually the title. Put it where it lands hardest, the first or last line of the chorus.
- Prosody: stable feelings (peace, resolution) suit perfect rhymes and settled lines; unstable ones (longing, doubt) suit near rhymes and lines that trail off. Keep verse lines a little more conversational and chorus lines simpler and bigger.
- The bridge is where to change the imagery: swap the verses' specific images for new ones while keeping its emotional job (reflection, shift, revelation).
- Avoid, unless you do it on purpose: stock phrases on autopilot, twisting word order to hit a rhyme, the same energy in every section, treating the first draft as sacred.
- Collect raw material first (images, puns, phrases), pick the best, then fit it into the structure. If a line must sit in a certain place, work the rhyme scheme backwards to set it up.

## Using the user's words

When the user pastes or attaches a poem, a draft or lyrics, those are the lyrics.

- Keep their lines, their order and their voice. Change only what a singer needs: add section labels; if there is no chorus, make one by repeating their strongest line or couplet; trim lines too long to sing; respell for pronunciation. Say in the reply what you changed.
- Write extra lines only if the structure needs them, in their vocabulary and imagery.
- Do not set a published song, or a recent poem by someone else, to music. Write an original song on the same theme and say why. An old poem in the public domain is fine.

## Parody, "to the tune of" and "in the style of"

- Write new lyrics with their own hook and phrasing. Do not reuse the original's lines, title or distinctive phrases.
- If the user will sing it to a known tune themselves, match the stress pattern and syllable count of the lines they describe, and say the generated track has its own melody, not the original's.
- "In the style of an artist" becomes genre, era, instruments, voice and mood. The artist's name stays out of the lyrics and the style prompt.

## Jingles (15 to 30 seconds)

- The name and the hook in the first line and again in the last.
- One benefit, and it must be one the user gave. Short words, a simple rhythm, and a tagline of 3 to 6 words that works spoken, without the music.
- End on the name. Add nothing the user did not give: no prices, awards, "best in town", years, addresses or phone numbers.

## Rap

- Verses of 12 to 16 lines in couplets, with a 4-line hook between them.
- Rhyme inside lines and across several syllables ("hesitation / celebration"). Vary the flow: short punchy lines against long run-on ones.
- In the style prompt say "rap vocal", the BPM and the beat ("boom-bap drums", "trap hi-hats").

## Other languages

Write natively in the language and rhyme the way songs in it do. Do not translate an English draft line by line. If the tool's description does not show that a model sings that language, say you are not sure the track will pronounce it well.

## Chords (only when asked)

Give a simple four-chord loop for each section in the key you chose, as a suggestion, and say the generated track does not follow it.
